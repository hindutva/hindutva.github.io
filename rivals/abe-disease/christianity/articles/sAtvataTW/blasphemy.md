The Catholics, Lutherans, Presbyterians, et al. could not even tolerate Mormons and Anabaptists who worship Jesus Christ. Why would they tolerate pagans? Countless people were burnt at the stake for "blaspheming Christ" or "blaspheming the Holy Spirit." 

Calvin and the Geneva Consistory had the Spanish physician Servetus burnt at the stake (indirectly via magistrate) because he likened the Trinity to three-headed Cerberus (one of the "Hounds of Hades").

Freedom of speech implies freedom to criticize religion and this includes criticism of even the most intolerant religions like Christianity and Islam.

## Early executions
Source: [TW](https://x.com/Saatvata/status/2107620857644568766)

In 330 AD, the Christian Roman Emperor Constantine had the pagan philosopher Canonaris beheaded at the Porticoes of Vigilantia (as per the Parastaseis syntomoi chronikai).

This was ~240 years before the birth of Muḥammad.

The Neoplatonist philosopher Sopater of Apamea was executed prior to 337 AD.

In 333 AD, Constantine issued a decree that officially commanded that all works written by the heretical theologian Arius be burned and that any Arian heretic who was caught concealing a work written by Arius would suffer capital punishment.

This was over 52 years before the execution of Priscillian of Ávila and the Synod of Trier and 237 years before the birth of Muḥammad.

Justinian introduced the death penalty for apostasy from Christianity to paganism.
He died around five year before Muḥammad's birth.

Chronologically, it's impossible for Islamic laws to have influenced the Codex Theodosianus, Codex Justinianus, etc.

## Ispiration for Islam?
On the contrary, it's possible that Byzantine law regarding blasphemy, heresy, disbelief, apostasy, etc. influenced early Islamic jurisprudence, especially after the conquest of Damascus/Dimašq.

Nearly all hadith collections, sirahs, works of fiqh, etc. postdate the conquest of Damascus.

The relationship between Islamic law and Byzantine Christian law is substantial enough to have been recognized as a scholarly question even though the nature, period, and extent of this influence is debated.

I would encourage you to read Joseph Schacht's "The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence."

Even if you're not entirely convinced by the arguments, it would broaden your perspective.

From a historical-critical perspective, the only work that can be attributed to Muḥammad with some certainty is the Qurʾān itself.
The earliest extant hadīṯ collections and earliest sīrah postdate Muḥammad's death.
The penalty for riddah in the Qurʾān seems to concern only the afterlife (2:217, 4:137).
No worldly/corpoeal penalties are mentioned.
It even mentions people who believe, fall into disbelief, believe again, and disbelieve further.
This is a rather peculiar statement if we assume that Muḥammad himself instituted the death penalty via apostasy (man baddala dīnahu fa-'qtulūhu).
It's possible that the statement was back-projected by political necessity after the Riddah wars.
